Endometriosis, three ways in

What the inherited risk variants might do, how a lesion makes its own oestrogen, and which immune cells are missing from the lining women shed each month. Three independent analyses of public data, each checked hard before I believed it.

  • Genetics
  • Epigenetics and hormones
  • Immunology

Genetics

What do the risk variants actually do?

Genome-wide association studies found the loci; the variants themselves are non-coding, so what they change is unknown. I used AlphaGenome, a model that predicts how a single DNA letter changes expression, splicing and local regulation, to score the eight replicated loci from the 2014 meta-analysis and rs3820282, the fine-mapped candidate at WNT4.

132extreme tracks for rs3820282, where chance predicts 13
10 of 10top GREB1 splicing changes sit on the variant's own junction

What I found

  • WNT4. The fine-mapped variant stands out about ten times above chance; the lead SNP beside it does not. The model predicts more oestrogen-receptor binding at the variant, matching a published knock-in mouse.
  • GREB1. The variant is the last base of an intron, and every one of the model's strongest splicing changes falls on that exact junction. The risk allele is the intact one, so the non-risk allele is the one predicted to disrupt it.
  • VEZT. The gene sits 17 kb from its variant, outside a small prediction window. In the full 1 Mb window its predicted change is small.

What changed my mind

My first screen ranked loci by each variant's single highest score and put GREB1 and VEZT on top. With thousands of tracks per variant, a top score near 1 is expected by chance, and VEZT wasn't inside that first window. Counting extreme tracks against chance gave the picture above.

Epigenetics and hormones

Does the lesion switch on its own oestrogen supply?

Aromatase, encoded by CYP19A1, turns androgens into oestrogen, and lesions grow on oestrogen. It is not a GWAS locus, which suggests any change happens in the lesion rather than being inherited. I reanalysed EndometDB (GSE141549), 336 samples across seven tissue types.

p = 0.00013ovarian endometrioma vs healthy endometrium (28 vs 42)
1.09×a modest but consistent shift

What I found

  • Only ovarian endometriomas stand out. They are higher than healthy endometrium and than every other lesion type, and the difference holds without the two most extreme samples and within the same menstrual cycle phase.
  • Patients' own endometrium shows nothing, whatever the disease stage.
  • One probe of two works. The flat one sits at the start of a single transcript version and barely correlates with the other (r = 0.05). CYP19A1 has two clusters of start sites about 95 kb apart, alternative promoters that could switch on in lesions.

The question I can't answer yet

The ovary itself makes aromatase. Endometrioma samples can include ovarian tissue, so part of the signal may come from follicles rather than lesion cells. Checking ovarian follicle markers is the next step.

Immunology

Which immune cells are missing from menstrual effluent?

Menstrual effluent carries the shed lining, so it could be a sample that needs no surgery. A single-cell study (GSE203191) reported fewer uterine NK cells and more B cells in endometriosis, testing cell by cell. I reproduced it counting each woman once: 8 controls, 11 diagnosed and 12 with symptoms but no diagnosis.

27% → 7.5%uterine NK1 cells, control to diagnosed, p = 0.017
2.6% → 6.7%B cells, p = 0.041, not significant after correction

What I found

  • The uNK1 reduction holds at the patient level, and agrees with a 2026 Oxford study that found fewer early NK cells in endometriosis.
  • The B-cell increase points the same way but doesn't survive correction.
  • Women with symptoms but no diagnosis sit in between.

Why the unit matters

Counting all 26,251 cells as independent gives p-values below 10−99. Counting each of the 19 women once gives 0.017 and 0.041. Same data; the honest answer comes from asking what the true sample is.

Where the three meet

Each lens points somewhere specific: an oestrogen-receptor switch at WNT4, an oestrogen-making enzyme raised in ovarian lesions, and an immune shift visible in shed tissue. Oestrogen runs through the first two, and menstrual effluent could tie the third to the first two without surgery.

What none of them can do alone is test it. The next step is the same for all three: real tissue, carefully phenotyped, from many more women.

It started at the bench

My first years in science were entirely wet lab. Cloning constructs, running RNAi screens, counting flies under a scope late at night. I loved the precision of it, and honestly, I still do.

Somewhere around my MSc the question I kept asking changed. It stopped being "what does this gene do" and turned into "how do we actually use what we already know." Fundamental research answers the first one. Translation and innovation answer the second, and that pull is where I keep ending up.

Most of what I build now lives at that junction, public datasets, models trained on someone else's sequencing run, tools that take a finding already sitting in a paper and push it one step closer to something usable. The wet lab habits never really left. They just moved into a notebook.

Wet Lab Experience

PCR-RFLP Genotyping of the VEGF rs699947 Polymorphism Across Human Populations

My first hands on wet lab experience started during my MSc. One of the first projects was analysing rs699947 SNP using a PCR based assay. For a course project, I optimized a PCR-RFLP assay to genotype rs699947—a VEGF promoter SNP linked to cancer, stroke, and neurodegeneration—and benchmarked our lab's 434-sample dataset against global reference data. Evaluating allele frequencies across populations revealed how subtle genetic variations influence personalized disease risk, while empirical troubleshooting exposed the limits of static DNA assays. Seeing how this single non-coding variant directly dictates VEGF transcript output became the pivotal spark that pulled me into RNA biology.

MSc dissertation - Using RNAi to manipulate mitochondrial function in Drosophila melanogaster

For my dissertation, I worked on understanding mitochondrial function through RNA interference. The plan was to restore Complex I activity in Drosophila by inserting the human gene NDUFS4 while silencing the fly ortholog ND-18 through RNAi, using the GAL4/UAS system and the GeneSwitch variant of it to build the fly lines, then reading out mRNA and protein levels alongside fly activity and oxygen consumption.

The human gene didn't show detectable expression in the ND-18-silenced line, most likely down to an error in how the fly crosses were set up rather than anything biological. But the silencing itself worked exactly as expected: flies with ND-18 knocked down showed reduced activity and respiration, confirming Complex I depletion took hold. A follow-up trial to properly test the rescue would be the obvious next step, and the underlying question, how much of Complex I function can be restored across species, still feels worth chasing.

Assisting on a CRISPR/dCas9 based epigenome editing

At the Max Planck Institute of Immunobiology and Epigenetics, I cloned and validated the GFP-tagged effector constructs for four target genes, Eggless, E(z), BAP111, and Snr1, using pJET vectors, PCR amplification, restriction digestion, ligation, bacterial transformation, and Sanger sequencing to confirm each construct's identity before it moved forward into co-transfection experiments in S2 cells and Drosophila. Epigenetics has always been fascinating and I was very excited to work in this lab and contribute my technical expertise. But I found myself wanting to connect this fundamental research out of the bench and into real world applications. While wet lab has enhanced my precision, my passion and appreciation for how our cell biology dances, I wanted to use that wisdom towards translational research.
